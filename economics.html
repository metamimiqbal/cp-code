Key Concepts
OC
Opportunity cost
Every choice means giving up the next-best option. Watching this video = not watching cat fails. That's your opportunity cost.
CA
Comparative advantage
Trade based on relative efficiency. Even if you're worse at everything, specialize in what you're least worst at. You're economically useful.
S&D
Supply & demand
Demand = what people want at a price. Supply = what producers make at a price. Where they meet is the equilibrium price.
IH
Invisible hand
Markets coordinate millions through prices & incentives — without any central planner. It's math + desire + desperation working in silence.
PC
Price controls
Government-set limits. Price ceiling → shortage. Price floor → surplus. Usually doesn't go well. Economics ignored is economics that bites back.

GDP & Growth
How we measure an economy, what makes it grow, and when it falls apart.

GDP Components (Typical Economy)
C — Consumption (you buying stuff)~60%
I — Investment (businesses building)~18%
G — Government spending~17%
X−M — Net exports~5%
3 Ways to Calculate GDP
①
Production approach
Sum value added at each stage. Don't count flour AND bread — just the bread. Otherwise you're double counting, and that's cheating.
②
Income approach
Sum all incomes earned: wages, profits, rents, interest. All roads lead to the same number.
③
Expenditure approach
C + I + G + (X − M). The most common. Add up every dollar spent in the economy.
What GDP Misses
Unpaid labor gap
Housework, caregiving, volunteering — all economically valuable, none counted in GDP.
Black market activity gap
Underground economies can be massive — especially in developing nations. Completely invisible to GDP.
Happiness & wellbeing gap
GDP doesn't care if you're happy. GDP from selling weapons counts the same as GDP from education.
GDP per capita better
GDP ÷ population. Fairer for comparing living standards. Still flawed — like a microwave with no door.
Inflation Types
D↑
Demand-pull
Demand outpaces supply → prices rise. Too much money chasing too few goods.
S↓
Cost-push
Supply collapses but demand holds → prices rise anyway. Oil shocks are the classic example.
☠️
Stagflation
Both demand AND supply collapse. High prices + recession. More existential dread than regular inflation.

Chapter 04
Trade & Currency
Why countries trade, how exchange rates work, and what happens when supply chains break.
Comparative Advantage — Worked Example
🇧🇷 Brazil

To grow 1 ton of coffee: give up 1 ton of corn

✓ Comparative advantage in coffee → export it
🇨🇦 Canada

To grow 1 ton of coffee: give up 2 tons of corn

✓ Comparative advantage in corn → export it
Both countries gain through specialization — even if one country is worse at producing both goods. Trade isn't about being the best; it's about being the least worst at the right thing.
Protectionism Tools
Tariffs (import tax) Quotas (quantity limits) Subsidies (help locals) Embargoes (full ban)
Protectionism shields domestic industries short-term — but raises prices and reduces efficiency in the long run. Politicians love it. Economists hate it.
Exchange Rates & Currency
FX
Forex market
Japan sells cars to US and wants yen, not dollars. Currencies get exchanged constantly. The forex market is the world's largest financial market.
↑↓
Floating vs fixed
Floating: moves with supply & demand (USD, EUR). Fixed: pegged to another currency or gold, maintained by force or willpower.
↓$
Depreciation
Your currency loses value → exports cheaper (good for trade), imports expensive (bad for buying stuff).
↑$
Appreciation
Your currency gains value → buy more foreign stuff (good), but your exports get expensive (bad for local producers).
Supply Chain Fragility
🚢 Suez Canal 2021

One ship (Ever Given) blocked 12% of global trade, delayed 400 ships, cost ~$10B/day. One boat. One canal.

💻 Chip shortage 2020

Semiconductor shortage made used cars more expensive than new ones. Supply shock from a few ultra-specialized factories in East Asia.

Chapter 05
Labor & Finance
Jobs as markets, wages, unemployment, stocks, bonds, and the risk-return trade-off.
Types of Unemployment
F
Frictional normal
Between jobs — temporarily searching for a better fit. Healthy part of a dynamic economy.
S
Structural skills gap
Skills mismatch — your job was automated, offshored, or made obsolete. Requires retraining.
C
Cyclical recession-driven
Demand drops in a downturn and firms cut staff. Disappears when the economy recovers.
Sc
Seasonal
Time-based patterns. Santa is unemployed in February. Ski instructors in summer.
💡 If you stop looking for work, you exit the unemployment statistics — you become "statistically invisible." The real rate is always higher than reported.
Financial Assets
S
Stocks ownership
Company slices. Rise on expectations, not just profits. Efficient market hypothesis: all info is already priced in — you can't consistently beat the market.
B
Bonds lending
Loans to governments or companies. Boring, but important. Bond prices fall when interest rates rise — that rule alone nukes economies.
D
Derivatives bets on bets
Options, futures, swaps. Useful for hedging risk. Also capable of causing the 2008 financial crisis.
C
Crypto volatile
Decentralized blockchain coins. Could be the future of money or a Ponzi scheme with extra steps. Mostly a casino disguised as innovation.
📌 Risk-return trade-off: Safe assets earn little. Risky ones might pay huge or nothing. Diversify. Don't put all eggs in one meme stock.
Chapter 06
Economic Systems
Capitalism, socialism, communism, and the mixed reality everyone actually lives in.
Capitalism

Private ownership, free markets, profit motive. The dominant global system.

✓ Innovation, efficiency, individual freedom
✗ Inequality, monopolies, market failures
Socialism

Collective ownership and redistribution. Society pools resources and shares benefits.

✓ Equality, social safety nets, healthcare
✗ Can reduce incentives, harder to scale
Communism

No private property, full state planning. Looks good on paper. Looks terrible in bread lines.

✓ Total equality in theory
✗ Famine (USSR grain quotas), inefficiency, authoritarianism
Mixed economy

The actual reality. Free markets + government intervention in different proportions.

✓ USA, China, India, Germany — all mixed
The US has subsidies, social security, and the TSA. China has billionaires.
Country Examples
🇸🇪
Scandinavia — democratic socialism
High taxes, strong social programs, but free markets still run most of the economy
🇺🇸
USA — market capitalism (with caveats)
Subsidies, public schools, Medicare, Social Security, the TSA. Definitely mixed.
🇨🇳
China — state capitalism
Government directs the economy strategically, but has billionaires and stock exchanges
🇯🇵
Japan — debt extreme
National debt over 260% of GDP — but stable because most creditors are domestic
The 3 Fundamental Economic Questions
🏭
What to make?
Which goods & services to produce given scarce resources
⚙️
How to make it?
Which production methods and technologies to use
🎯
Who gets it?
How output is distributed across the population
Every economic system — capitalism, socialism, communism, mixed — is just a different answer to these three questions. No one has nailed it perfectly yet.
Chapter 07
Behavioral Economics
Traditional economics assumes humans are rational. LOL. Real humans are chaos merchants.
Cognitive Biases
HD
Hyperbolic discounting
$5 now feels better than $50 later. We dramatically over-weight the present moment. This is why you eat the cake and skip the gym.
LA
Loss aversion
Losing $20 hurts more than gaining $20 feels good. Losses loom about twice as large as equivalent gains psychologically.
AB
Anchoring bias
If I say a phone costs $10,000 first, then $999 suddenly feels like a steal. First numbers we see become anchors — even irrelevant ones.
HB
Herd behavior
Everyone panic-buys toilet paper → so do you. We follow crowds, especially in uncertainty. Markets crash partly because of herd behavior.
SC
Sunk cost fallacy
Sitting through a terrible Marvel sequel because "I already watched the first eight." Past costs shouldn't affect future decisions — but they do.
Nudges — Design for Better Decisions
Default options
Auto-enroll people in retirement savings → participation rates skyrocket. We go with the default. Change the default, change the behavior.
Loss framing
"You'll save $200" vs "You're losing $200 by not doing this" — same facts, wildly different response rates. Loss framing wins because of loss aversion.
Social proof
"9 out of 10 of your neighbors pay their taxes on time" → compliance goes up. We look to others to know what's normal.
Commitment devices
Locking your future self into good behavior. Savings accounts you can't touch. Pre-commitments to gym classes you've already paid for.
Key insight
Traditional economics: humans are perfectly rational utility-maximizers. Behavioral economics: humans are predictably irrational. Policy should account for real human behavior, not the idealized version. That's why governments and apps nudge us — because left alone, we'd spend our retirement fund on Uber Eats.
