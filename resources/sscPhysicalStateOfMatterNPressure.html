States of Matter — Overview

Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. Based on the arrangement and energy of its constituent particles (atoms/molecules), matter exists in different physical states.

🔬 Interactive Particle Simulation

Watch how particles behave differently in each state

🧊 Solid

  • Definite shape & volume
  • Particles closely packed
  • Very low kinetic energy
  • Incompressible

💧 Liquid

  • Definite volume, no fixed shape
  • Particles close but mobile
  • Moderate kinetic energy
  • Nearly incompressible

💨 Gas

  • No definite shape or volume
  • Particles far apart & fast
  • High kinetic energy
  • Highly compressible

⚡ Plasma (4th State)

  • Ionized gas — free electrons
  • Found in: Sun, lightning, neon lights
  • Extremely high energy
  • Most common state in universe

Intermolecular Forces & Kinetic Theory

Intermolecular Forces

  • Cohesion — attraction between like molecules (e.g. water–water)
  • Adhesion — attraction between unlike molecules (e.g. water–glass)
  • These forces determine state of matter
  • Weakest in gases, strongest in solids

Kinetic Theory of Matter

  • All matter is made of particles in motion
  • Higher temperature → greater KE
  • KE of particles determines the state
  • Average KE ∝ Absolute Temperature (K)

Surface Tension

The tendency of a liquid surface to contract to the smallest possible area due to cohesive forces among surface molecules is called surface tension.

💡 Unit of Surface Tension: N/m (Newton per metre). Example: Water droplets form spheres — minimum surface area for maximum volume.
  • Causes insects to walk on water (water striders)
  • Mercury forms convex meniscus (cohesion > adhesion)
  • Water forms concave meniscus (adhesion > cohesion)
  • Surface tension decreases with increase in temperature

Viscosity

Viscosity is the resistance of a fluid to flow — the internal friction between layers of a moving fluid.

  • Honey has high viscosity; water has low viscosity
  • Viscosity of liquids decreases with temperature
  • Viscosity of gases increases with temperature
  • Unit: Pa·s (Pascal-second)

🌡️ Latent Heat

Heat absorbed or released during a change of state without change in temperature.

  • Latent heat of fusion — solid to liquid
  • Latent heat of vaporization — liquid to gas
  • For water: Lfusion = 334 kJ/kg
  • For water: Lvap = 2260 kJ/kg

📊 Important Temperature Points

  • Melting point of water: 0°C (273 K)
  • Boiling point of water: 100°C (373 K)
  • Boiling point decreases at higher altitudes
  • Pressure affects boiling point (Clausius–Clapeyron)
  • Freezing point depression by dissolving solutes

Pressure — Definition & Formula

Pressure is defined as the force applied perpendicularly per unit area of a surface.

P = F / A
P = Pressure (Pa)  |  F = Force (N)  |  A = Area (m²)

SI Unit: Pascal (Pa) = 1 N/m²   Named after Blaise Pascal

Other units: bar, atm, mmHg, psi

1 atm = 101,325 Pa ≈ 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa = 760 mmHg = 76 cm Hg

⚡ Pressure vs Area — Interactive

Why sharp objects cut easily?

A knife has a very small area. For the same force, pressure = F/A is very large → easier cutting.

Same force + smaller area = MORE pressure

Why snowshoes / camel feet?

Large flat feet spread the weight over a greater area → smaller pressure → don't sink into snow/sand.

Same force + larger area = LESS pressure

Pressure in Liquids (Fluid Pressure)

A liquid exerts pressure in all directions. The pressure at a point in a static liquid depends on:

P = ρ g h
ρ = density of liquid (kg/m³)  |  g = 9.8 m/s²  |  h = depth (m)

🌊 Pressure Increases with Depth

Drag the slider to see pressure at different depths

Depth: 50 cm

Properties of Liquid Pressure

  • Increases with depth
  • Increases with density of liquid
  • Acts in all directions
  • Independent of shape of container
  • Independent of area of cross-section
  • Horizontal pressure same at equal depths

Hydrostatic Paradox

The pressure at the bottom of a liquid depends only on height (h) and density — not on the shape or volume of the container.

Three containers with different shapes but same height of water → same pressure at bottom!

Pressure Due to a Liquid Column

If a liquid column of height h and density ρ rests on area A:

P = ρgh    and    Total Pressure = P_atm + ρgh
Total pressure at depth h = Atmospheric pressure + Pressure due to liquid column

Atmospheric Pressure & Barometer

The Earth is surrounded by a layer of air called the atmosphere. The weight of this column of air exerts pressure on the Earth's surface called atmospheric pressure.

How it works

  • A glass tube ~1m long, sealed at one end
  • Filled with mercury and inverted in a trough
  • Mercury column stays at 76 cm height
  • Atmospheric pressure supports this column
  • P₀ = ρHg × g × h = 13600 × 9.8 × 0.76
  • = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa ✓
At higher altitudes, mercury column height decreases because atmospheric pressure is lower.

Aneroid Barometer

Uses a sealed flexible metallic box instead of mercury. More portable and convenient. Used in altimeters and weather forecasting instruments.

Manometer

A U-tube device used to measure gauge pressure (pressure above atmospheric). Used in labs and industry. Can use mercury or water as the manometric fluid.

Effects & Applications of Atmospheric Pressure

  • Suction cups work due to atm. pressure
  • Drinking through a straw (creating low pressure)
  • Syringe — drawing liquid upward
  • Water pump — lifting water from wells
  • Altimeter in aircraft measures altitude
  • Weather forecasting (high/low pressure)
  • Pressure cooker — increases boiling point
  • Mountain sickness at high altitude

Pascal's Law & Hydraulic Machines

Pascal's Law (1648)

"Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions throughout the fluid and acts on every unit area."

P₁ = P₂   →   F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂
F₁ = Input force  |  A₁ = Input area  |  F₂ = Output force  |  A₂ = Output area

⚙️ Hydraulic System Animation

Small force on small piston → large force on big piston

⟺ fluid ⟺

F₂ = 30 N

Mechanical advantage = A₂/A₁ — same pressure, more force on larger area

🚗 Hydraulic Brake

Small foot pressure → transmitted through brake fluid → large force on brake pads → stops vehicle.

Uses incompressibility of liquids and Pascal's Law

🏗️ Hydraulic Jack/Lift

Used in garages to lift cars. Small force on small piston creates large upward force on large piston.

F₂ = F₁ × (A₂/A₁)

💉 Syringe

Pascal's law is also applied in syringes — pressure applied on plunger transmits to push out liquid uniformly.

✈️ Aircraft Controls

Hydraulic systems control ailerons, flaps, and landing gear in aircraft using the principle of transmitted pressure.

Archimedes' Principle & Buoyancy

Archimedes' Principle

"When a body is wholly or partially immersed in a fluid, it experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid displaced."

F_b = ρ_fluid × V_displaced × g
F_b = Buoyant force | ρ = density of fluid | V = volume of fluid displaced | g = 9.8 m/s²

Conditions of Floating

  • Floats: density of object < density of fluid
  • Sinks: density of object > density of fluid
  • Neutral buoyancy: densities equal (hovers)
A steel ship floats because its average density (including hollow parts) is less than water!

Applications

  • Ships & submarines — buoyancy design
  • Hydrometer — measures fluid density
  • Hot air balloon — buoyancy in air
  • Fish swim bladder — adjusts buoyancy
  • Life jackets — increase volume → increase buoyant force

Apparent Weight

When an object is immersed in a liquid, it appears lighter because the buoyant force acts upward:

Apparent Weight = True Weight − Buoyant Force
W_apparent = W − F_b = mg − ρ_fluid × V × g
This is why objects feel lighter underwater — used in determining density by measuring loss of weight!

Density & Relative Density

Density

Mass per unit volume of a substance.

ρ = m / V
Unit: kg/m³  |  Also g/cm³

Density of water = 1000 kg/m³ = 1 g/cm³

Relative Density (Specific Gravity)

Ratio of density of a substance to density of water at 4°C.

R.D. = ρ_substance / ρ_water
Dimensionless (no unit)

R.D. of mercury = 13.6 (mercury is 13.6× denser than water)

📊 Density of Common Substances

Measuring Density Using Archimedes' Principle

  1. Measure true weight in air: W₁
  2. Measure apparent weight in water: W₂
  3. Loss of weight = Buoyant force = W₁ − W₂
  4. Relative Density = W₁ / (W₁ − W₂)
R.D. = W_air / (W_air − W_water)
